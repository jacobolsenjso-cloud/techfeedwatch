Print catalogs were once considered a relic of pre-digital commerce. They are now proving to be powerful tools in modern omnichannel retail. Far from being mere brand awareness pieces, these physical publications can directly drive online sales. They can also deepen customer engagement when integrated thoughtfully into a digital strategy. The key lies in understanding actual customer behavior and using automation to create impactful, data-driven print experiences.

## Re-evaluating Print in a Digital-First World

Many businesses, particularly those with a strong digital focus, initially view print catalogs with skepticism. The prevailing mindset often prioritizes conversion rates, AI, and e-commerce sales, seeing print as an outdated channel. This perspective overlooks the unique role print can play, especially for certain product categories and customer segments. For a wholesaler of seasonal decorations, for instance, products like Christmas bobbles or mugs are often impulse purchases. Customers do not typically search for these items online. In such cases, a physical catalog can be highly effective. These B2B customers are often mid-50s female owners of seasonal stores. They place large orders, with an average shopping basket around €1,000. Webshop sales are substantial annually. Another 10 million comes through classical channels like phone orders and key accounts. This highlights the continued importance of diverse touchpoints.

## The High Cost of Traditional Catalog Production

Before automation, producing a single print catalog was an incredibly labor-intensive and expensive undertaking. A typical Christmas catalog featured 5,000 articles across 3656 pages. This required two full-time graphic artists working for three months. This manual process involved individually placing each item on pages. It also meant editing article numbers and detailing volume and material information. The effort amounted to over 640 hours of work per catalog. Once printed, a single catalog could weigh 1.7 kilograms, with 8,000 copies distributed across Europe. The sheer scale of production, shipping, and printing costs made it a major investment. This often led to a desire for greater efficiency. Businesses searched for ways to reduce the manual burden.

## Automating Catalog Creation: Efficiency Meets Inspiration

The drive to modernize catalog production led to exploring automation. The aim was to reduce manual effort and generate catalogs more efficiently. The goal was to move from a process requiring hundreds of hours. It aimed for one that could produce a catalog "at the push of a button." This involved creating templates and using specialized software that could convert product data into a print-ready format.

A critical step in this automation was data curation. Raw data exported from an online shop often resulted in a "wild" and disorganized layout. Categories were mixed and related products scattered. To achieve an appealing and functional catalog, data had to be carefully prepared. This included sorting products into curated ranges and assortments, standardizing sizes, and harmonizing titles. Once the data was clean and structured, AI tools could assist in generating layouts. This streamlined approach allowed for major time savings in production. This time could then be reinvested into enhancing the physical quality of the catalog. For example, businesses could select premium paper and finishes. This created a more tactile and emotionally resonant product.

## Customer Behavior: The Real Driver of Catalog Design

Initial attempts at automated catalog design were driven by a digital-first perspective. They often focused on creating "inspirational" layouts. These layouts featured product collections based on color or theme, rather than traditional categories. The assumption was that customers would use the online shop for detailed categorization. The catalog would merely serve as a visual touchpoint. However, customer feedback quickly revealed this assumption was incorrect. Customers found these "chaos" layouts difficult to work with, preferring the clear categorization they were accustomed to.

This feedback highlighted a basic insight. Print catalogs are not just inspirational brochures. They are often used as practical work tools by customers. They expect to find products organized logically, similar to how they navigate an online store. This realization led to reintroducing traditional categories into the print catalog. It balanced inspirational sections with clear, structured product listings. And, integrating scannable EN codes into the catalog allowed customers to easily bridge the gap between print and digital. They could add products directly to their online shopping carts. This direct link between the physical catalog and the digital storefront proved to be a powerful driver of online conversion.

## Print Catalogs as E-commerce Tools

Beyond direct conversion, print catalogs serve multiple strategic functions within an omnichannel retail strategy. They can act as a seasonal trigger. New catalogs signal the start of ordering periods for upcoming holidays like Christmas or Easter. They also provide a tangible experience that can be monetized. For example, a company successfully reduced its catalog circulation. They offered the catalog for sale at €25, bundled with a voucher redeemable online. This not only subsidized production costs. It also attracted new customers, including B2C buyers who simply wanted a physical Christmas catalog.

The enduring appeal of print also extends to digital formats. Despite the rise of sophisticated webshops, many customers still actively browse digital flip-HTML versions of PDF catalogs. This technology has been around for 15 years. This shows that customer preferences for how they consume product information can be diverse. These preferences are sometimes counter-intuitive to digital natives. In the end, the customer decides how they want to engage with both print and digital versions. Retailers must adapt their strategies to meet these varied behaviors.

## The Enduring Power of Emotion and Brand in Retail

The experience with print catalogs also offers broader lessons for the future of e-commerce. This is particularly true concerning the role of AI and "agentic commerce." While AI can automate many basic processes, it struggles to replicate the emotional connection. It also struggles with the decision-making inherent in many purchases. For products with an emotional appeal, like seasonal decorations, customers often seek the feeling of reassurance. They also seek the dopamine release that comes from making a personal choice.

Retailers must be careful not to become mere "AIPs" (AI Product/Service Providers) that simply feed data to large AI platforms. Instead, they need to cultivate their own data. They must build direct communication channels within their online shops. And they must strengthen their brand identity. Trust, emotions, and brand awareness remain vital on a human level. The future of retail will likely involve a blend of efficient automation for routine tasks. It will also have a strong focus on human-centric experiences. These experiences foster emotional connections and brand loyalty. This ensures the retailer maintains a central role in the customer's purchasing journey.
