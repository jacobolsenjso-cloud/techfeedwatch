AI is rapidly transforming how businesses operate, shifting from broad applications to highly specialized, integrated solutions. This evolution centers on AI agents, which are becoming central figures in how software interacts with enterprise systems and even the broader internet. This transition demands a fundamental rethinking of digital infrastructure, product design, and business models to accommodate these new digital workers.

## The Agent-First Future
The digital world is approaching a major turning point where AI agents are poised to become the primary users of software. Experts suggest that the number of agents could soon exceed human users by 100x or even 1,000x. This projection highlights a critical mismatch: the internet's current architecture, including web standards, pricing models, and enterprise systems, was designed for human interaction. For AI agents to function effectively at this scale, a complete re-evaluation of how software is built and deployed is necessary.

Agents operate differently from humans. They do not attend webinars or respond to outbound emails. Instead, they require systems that are API-first, meaning they can interact directly with software through programmatic interfaces. They also need structured data and clean codebases to understand and execute tasks accurately. Up-to-date documentation is essential for agents to navigate complex workflows and processes. The guiding principle for developers is evolving from "make something people want" to "make something agents want." This shift impacts not only product design but also how businesses approach their go-to-market strategies and revenue generation.

## Rebuilding Digital Infrastructure for AI
The current state of digital infrastructure presents varying levels of friction for AI agents. In the business-to-business (B2B) sector, many companies have already developed well-developed APIs. These interfaces allow different business systems to communicate, making them naturally compatible with AI agents. Companies like Box, for instance, have command-line interfaces (CLIs) that work well for agents, as they aim to be platforms for other businesses to build upon.

However, the consumer side of the market faces major challenges. Consumer companies, publishers, and e-commerce websites have a long history, spanning 20 years, of blocking bots. This historical approach, while understandable for security and spam prevention, now hinders the integration of beneficial AI agents. For example, an agent cannot easily buy items on Amazon, pay a credit card bill, or access a subscription article from a publication like The Information or Wall Street Journal without navigating a human-designed website.

A major disincentive for consumer-facing entities to adapt quickly is their reliance on advertising revenue. When an agent browses a website on behalf of a human, there are no human "eyeballs" viewing ads. This means publishers and e-commerce sites do not generate ad revenue from agent interactions. While large companies like Amazon might eventually adapt due to the sheer volume of agent activity, many others may resist until the economic pressure of lost sales from non-adapted agents becomes too great. The problem is being tackled from two directions: agent companies are approaching businesses to expose APIs, while browser control technologies are improving, though they remain flaky and slow.

## The Enterprise Documentation Imperative
Within the enterprise, a critical hurdle for widespread agent adoption is the quality and availability of internal documentation. Historically, if a standard operating procedure or workflow was not well-documented, employees could often rely on colleagues for answers. This informal knowledge transfer, while sometimes inefficient, provided a workaround.

For AI agents, however, this lack of explicit documentation is a recipe for failure. Agents require clear, structured information to understand processes, context, and guardrails. Without it, they can "flail," unable to complete objectives accurately or autonomously. Companies that fail to document their processes are inherently inefficient, as expertise housed solely within human employees can be lost when those employees leave. Complete documentation is not just good practice; it is essential for "onboarding" new agents and ensuring they can use the vast amount of knowledge and expertise accumulated within an organization. While agents possess some intelligence to figure things out, providing them with detailed documentation greatly shortcuts their learning process and improves their effectiveness.

## The Rise of Personal and Orchestrated AI
The concept of personal AI assistants has gained major traction, driven by innovative projects and widespread adoption. Open Claw, an open-source agent developed by Peter Steinberger, became incredibly popular, accumulating 200,000 GitHub stars and even causing a sell-out of Mac mini computers as users sought local hardware to run it. Its success led to its acquisition by OpenAI and inspired many companies to develop similar solutions.

Perplexity, for example, launched "Perplexity Computer," a cloud-based agent designed to orchestrate 20 different AI models to execute complex, multi-step workflows autonomously. This system can build a plan, spin up sub-agents, and run for hours to achieve an objective. Following this, Perplexity introduced "Personal Computer," software that runs persistently on a Mac mini, directly responding to the trend set by Open Claw. Other major players like Tencent, Cohere, and Cursor Automations have also entered this space, indicating a broad industry shift towards personal AI assistants.

While these tools represent a major leap forward, their accessibility for the average non-technical user remains a point of discussion. Open Claw, being an open-source project requiring installation, is generally not suitable for a casual user. Perplexity Personal Computer aims for wider appeal, but some believe it still presents too much of a hurdle for truly non-technical people. These advanced agents are currently seen as most powerful within work contexts, helping professionals get tasks done, rather than becoming a widespread tool for everyday personal use cases beyond simple question-answering.

## Slack as an Operating System for Agents
As AI agents become more prevalent, existing enterprise platforms are adapting to serve as their operational hubs. Slack, a communication platform used by 47 million people daily, is evolving its vision to become an "operating system for the agentic enterprise." This means Slack aims to manage and help the interactions of AI agents within a company's workflow.

The analogy to an operating system is apt: just as an OS obscures the complexity of hardware from human users, Slack intends to obscure the complexity of underlying AI models and systems from both human users and agents. It provides a standardized environment where agents can access tools, data, and collaborate, making their integration smoother and more efficient. This strategic shift positions Slack not merely as a messaging tool, but as a central nervous system for AI-driven productivity and automation within organizations.
