A computational universe suggests that reality, at its most fundamental level, operates like a vast program, generating all observed phenomena from simple, underlying computational rules. This perspective shifts how we understand the origins of complexity in nature, moving away from explanations rooted in randomness or elaborate initial conditions towards an emergent computational process.

Steven Wolfram, who Lex Fridman notes is making his third appearance on the podcast, is the founder of Wolfram Research—the company behind Mathematica, Wolfram Alpha, and Wolfram Language—and has been at the forefront of this exploration for decades. His work began in the early 1980s, seeking to understand "what secret does nature have" that allows it to produce complicated forms like snowflakes or galaxies, or how "living systems get produced," a question he first became interested in some 50 years ago. This exploration culminated in his book A New Kind of Science, published almost 20 years ago, where he presented a study of complexity and an approach for modeling complex systems. He found that traditional mathematical physics struggled to model this emergent complexity. Wolfram realized that "programs are really good source" of raw material for creating models. Instead of complex, purpose-built code, he focused on the "computational Universe of possible programs"—the simplest programs imaginable—to observe their behavior. This led him to cellular automata, systems where simple rules dictate how cells in a grid evolve, often producing unexpected complexity. A prime example is Rule 30, a particular cellular automaton rule that, starting from a single black cell, generates a "really complicated pattern." This challenged the common assumption that "simple rules simple behavior," a key discovery about the computational universe.

## What a Computational Universe Reveals about Reality

The profound insight derived from these simple programs is what Steven Wolfram calls "computational irreducibility." This means that for systems like Rule 30, predicting their state after "a million steps" often requires actually running the simulation for a million steps. There is no shortcut or compressed formula to jump ahead and determine the outcome without equivalent computational effort. This phenomenon mirrors the behavior of numbers like Pi; as proved in the 1800s, Pi is "not a rational number," meaning its digits don't repeat, and predicting specific properties of its digit sequence, such as the equal frequency of all digits ("0 1 2 3" or whatever), remains unsolved. This contrasts with earlier ideas in dynamical systems theory, particularly "chaos theory" which became prominent in the 1980s, exemplified by the "shift map" (taking "2x mod one"). These systems often amplified randomness in initial conditions. In a computational universe, complexity isn't merely filtering external randomness; it's intrinsically generated from deterministic, simple rules. This finding has profound implications for how science views everything from fundamental physics to economics and biology, suggesting a foundational multi-computational model at play across diverse domains. The ideas culminating in the Wolfram Physics Project, launched about a year and a half ago, push this concept further. This project proposes that space itself is not an empty void but is "made of something," akin to how water is made of molecules, producing fluid flow. This "something" is "atoms of space," linked in a hypergraph, with their identity like "UUIDs" in computing. Particles such as an electron or a photon are viewed as "tangles in the structure of space," emerging from these fundamental interactions. The elementary length of these "atoms of space" is hypothesized to be incredibly small, perhaps on the order of "10-100 m," far smaller than a proton's "10- 15 M" or the smallest observable scale of "10- 21 M." This perspective offers a novel way to address the question of "why does the universe exist" by seeing it as an emergent, self-actualizing computation, where what we perceive is a feature of this evolving structure.

### The Bottom Line

The notion of a computational universe, rooted in the unexpected complexity arising from simple programs, offers a revolutionary framework for understanding natural phenomena and fundamental physics. It shifts our scientific lens from seeking hidden randomness or intricate designs to exploring how basic computational rules can intrinsically generate the universe's vast complexity. This perspective suggests that everything we experience, from the shape of galaxies to the existence of elementary particles like electrons, could be emergent properties of an underlying multi-computational system. The implications extend beyond physics, offering a unified model that could inform diverse scientific fields by applying insights gained from fundamental computational principles.
