## What Is Individual Sovereignty, And How Does It Manifest?

Individual sovereignty describes the inherent authority a person possesses, which can be generally defined as 'the authority to act as you see fit,' encompassing both their capacity for choice and their self-determination. Philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in his 1762 book *The Social Contract*, famously asserted that "man is Born Free and everywhere he's in Chains," articulating a foundational tension between innate liberty and societal constraints. At its core, sovereignty is about who holds ultimate power, and how that power is distributed across individuals and institutions.

Historically, sovereignty was often absolute and centralized. In ancient Egypt, for example, the Pharaoh exercised complete sovereignty, with everyone else operating according to his interests. Modern Western democracies represent a shift towards decentralized sovereignty, where citizens participate in electing officials who make decisions on their behalf. However, the concept extends beyond political structures, deeply intertwining with personal agency. Victor Frankl, whose insights are famously captured in his book *Man's Search for Meaning*, described an internal form of sovereignty as "the final human freedom"—the power to choose one's response to any situation, irrespective of external pressures. This internal fortitude suggests that while external forces can impose limits, an individual's mental and emotional autonomy remains a potent domain of sovereignty.

The ability to manipulate one's environment and interact with others is a key aspect of this autonomy, with roots in human evolution. The adoption of an upright stance freed human hands from locomotion, allowing for greater dexterity and the development of opposable thumbs. This enhanced capacity to manipulate and particularize the environment, alongside the co-evolution of fine musculature in the face and tongue for speech, laid the groundwork for complex tool-making and organized cooperation. Money, in this context, emerges as a sophisticated "tech layer" or tool that facilitates trade and acts as an extension of the mind, enabling individuals to handle a complex world by compressing data and representing value. Much like the fascination with advanced AI systems such as Tesla autopilot, these technologies extend human capabilities and reshape our interaction with the environment. However, this connection also means that central bodies capable of manipulating money can subtly influence individual thought and action.

A critical expression of individual sovereignty lies in property rights. Property is not merely an asset; it is, as Robert Breedlove, host of the *What is Money* podcast, explains, "the socially acknowledged relationship between a human and an asset such that you have exclusive rights and responsibilities to a particular asset." This definition highlights property as a form of information—a system of recognized claims and duties, not just the physical item itself. These rights are fundamental, rooted in natural law, encompassing "rights to life liberty and property." This framework underpins capitalism, a system where, as Lex Fridman points out, "each individual has the exclusive rights to the fruits of their labor." The "first capitalist in the world" might have been a caveman who dug a shelter and claimed it, while the "first communist or socialist" would be someone who violently encroached upon that claim. This distinction illuminates the core difference: capitalism grants individuals exclusive rights to their creations, while communism or socialism implies that others, typically the state, hold some rights to the value an individual creates.

## How Does Society Affect Personal Sovereignty?

While individual sovereignty implies inherent personal freedom, its practical manifestation is constantly shaped by societal structures, particularly economic ones. The existence of economic surplus, first seen with the agricultural age's ability to cultivate crops and generate savings, led to the emergence of government. Government, in this view, functions as a protection-producing enterprise for the economic surplus generated by a trading society, ensuring non-violent dispute resolution and the rule of law. The division of labor, a common economics term, arises when individuals specialize (e.g., one person making hats, another making boots) and trade, creating a positive-sum game where everyone benefits. This economic efficiency enhances collective well-being but also introduces complexities regarding the exercise of individual sovereignty within a shared system.

The challenge to individual sovereignty often arises when these protective institutions overstep their original mandate. Governments, often holding a monopolistic position as security guards for the economy, may begin to abuse their power. A significant avenue for this abuse is the monopolization of money. When a central body controls the money supply, it can effectively control people's energy, perceptions, and ultimately, their freedom of action. For example, some billionaires can "negotiate your own tax treaty" to secure favorable tax treatment, highlighting how substantial financial power can reshape the rules, even allowing individuals to "Lobby Congress" and influence laws.

Inflation serves as a stark example of how centralized control over money erodes individual sovereignty. While often presented as a normal part of a healthy economy, inflation, as Robert Breedlove argues, is "theft integrated into the money." It functions as a "tech backdoor," allowing a select few to siphon value from others, gradually diminishing purchasing power. This subtle, systemic siphoning of wealth represents a significant challenge to individual economic sovereignty, affecting everyone who cannot create money themselves. This issue stands at the heart of many modern economies, including the US, which despite priding itself on free-market capitalism, operates with an "anti-capitalistic institution" in the form of the Central Bank.

The potential for such monopolization has fueled arguments for alternative systems. Michael Malice, a proponent of anarchism, contends that any amount of government will eventually expand to control power and money. The historical record shows that even nations like the US, which "outcompeted communism in the 20th century," still face the challenge of a centralized monetary system. However, the advent of technologies like Bitcoin has introduced a new dimension to this discussion. Satoshi Nakamoto’s innovation made it possible, "for the first time in history," to have money that "cannot be monopolized, cannot be corrupted, cannot be changed... cannot be weaponized." This technological shift offers a potential pathway to bolster individual economic sovereignty by removing the ability of a central authority to manipulate the monetary system, thereby providing individuals with greater control over the value they create. Questions about authority in new territories, such as "Elon Musk and Mars" with SpaceX suggesting that "nobody on Earth has any Authority on mars," also highlight the ongoing evolution of how property and governance are defined. ## What To Actually Do

Understanding individual sovereignty requires active engagement with the systems that govern our lives. Recognize that your sovereignty is not just an abstract philosophical concept but is actively shaped by your economic choices and societal structures.

First, cultivate your internal sovereignty by consciously exercising your "final human freedom" to choose your responses to circumstances. This internal resilience is a bedrock for all other forms of freedom. Second, understand the nature of money and property. Money is a tool, but when controlled centrally, it can become a mechanism of control. Educate yourself on how monetary policy, such as that enacted by a Central Bank, can impact your purchasing power and economic autonomy. This includes recognizing inflation for what it is—a subtle form of value erosion—rather than simply an unavoidable economic phenomenon.

Third, engage with financial systems critically. Explore decentralized alternatives that promise to return control over value to individuals, as technologies like Bitcoin aim to do. While specific investments are not advice, understanding the principles behind these technologies can empower you to make more informed decisions about where you store your value and how you interact with the economy. Finally, practice what is known as the "division of labor" in your own life, recognizing that specialization and trade enhance efficiency and create collective value. Protecting the fruits of your labor, both intellectual and material, is a direct assertion of your individual sovereignty. By understanding the mechanisms that either support or undermine your authority to act as you see fit, you equip yourself to make more sovereign choices in a world that often seeks to constrain them.
