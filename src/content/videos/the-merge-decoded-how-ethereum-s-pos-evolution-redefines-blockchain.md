Ethereum's operational shift, known as The Merge, moved the network from Proof of Work (PoW) to Proof of Stake (PoS). This happened in September 2022. This change fundamentally altered how new blocks are added and secured. It replaced energy-intensive mining with a system where participants stake their ETH as collateral.

## From Proof of Work to Proof of Stake

When Ethereum launched in 2015, it used a Proof of Work consensus mechanism. This system is similar to Bitcoin's. Under Proof of Work, miners competed to solve complex computational puzzles. They used specialized hardware, often powerful graphics cards, and a large amount of electricity. The miner who solved the puzzle first proposed the next block of transactions. Other nodes then verified this block. The winning miner received ETH as a reward. This process secured the network. However, it required continuous, intensive computational work.

Proof of Stake had been a long-term goal for Ethereum. The challenge was changing the consensus system for a live network. This network already held valuable assets and applications. Ethereum prepared for this transition by launching the Beacon Chain in December 2020. The Beacon Chain ran the Proof of Stake mechanism in parallel with the existing Proof of Work network. This allowed for testing of validators and staking. Regular transactions continued on the original chain during this period.

The Merge then happened in September 2022. Ethereum's execution layer managed accounts, smart contracts, and transactions. It then connected to the Beacon Chain's consensus mechanism. Mining stopped completely. The network's transaction history continued without interruption. Users did not need to swap their existing ETH for a new currency.

## The Mechanics of Validation

Proof of Stake replaces miners with validators. To run a validator directly, a participant must deposit 32 ETH. Smaller ETH holders can still participate. They can use staking pools or services. However, these options carry additional risks. These include custody risks and smart contract risks.

The protocol assigns tasks to validators. Validators take turns proposing new blocks of transactions. They also confirm blocks proposed by others. Ethereum calls these confirmations "attestations" or "certificates." The validator's staked ETH acts as collateral. This gives them something to lose if they break important consensus rules.

Validators who perform their duties correctly earn rewards. Staying offline might result in smaller penalties. More severe misconduct leads to a penalty called "slashing." For example, signing conflicting versions of the blockchain is serious misconduct. Slashing destroys a portion of the validator's staked ETH. It also removes them from the network. This only happens to validators who try to break the rules.

## Enhanced Security and Sustainability

The shift to Proof of Stake significantly changed the cost of attacking Ethereum. In a Proof of Work system, an attacker needs control over mining hardware and electricity. They must acquire enough resources to overpower honest miners. This is known as a 51% attack. They need to control more than 51% of the network's total computational power.

In a Proof of Stake system, a 51% attack requires control over a very large amount of staked ETH. An attacker would need more than 51% of the total staked ETH. This makes such an attack extremely expensive. It would require acquiring a vast sum of ETH to stake. The protocol would also allow for the slashing of an attacker's stake. This further deters malicious behavior.

The Merge drastically cut Ethereum's electricity consumption. The network no longer relies on continuous computational work from miners. This greatly reduces its environmental footprint. The Merge also ended the regular issuance of new ETH to Proof of Work miners. This changed the network's economic model.

## What The Merge Did Not Change

While these changes were fundamental, The Merge did not automatically make Ethereum transactions cheaper. It also did not significantly increase the number of transactions the network could process. Transaction fees still depend on the demand for block space. When demand is high, fees increase.

Faster and less expensive activity typically comes from subsequent scaling efforts. These are often called Layer 2 networks. Layer 2 solutions process transactions off the main Ethereum chain. They then post the results back to the main chain. This helps to ease congestion and lower costs on the primary network.

## Trade-offs and Centralization Concerns

Proof of Stake also involves trade-offs. Large cryptocurrency exchanges and staking providers can control significant amounts of staked ETH. This concentration of staked assets is a point of concern. While Ethereum's design encourages many independent validators, the focus of staked ETH is worth monitoring.

Users who participate in collective or liquid staking might rely on additional companies or smart contracts. This introduces potential risks. These include the security of the third-party service or the smart contract code.

## The Role of Staking in Governance

It is important to understand what staking does not do. Owning ETH does not give a person a direct vote on every change to Ethereum's software. Instead, developers propose upgrades to the network. Node operators then choose which software to run. Validators participate in consensus based on those rules.

Staking secures the production of new blocks. It does not transform Ethereum into a simple system of shareholder voting. Ethereum still needs thousands of independent participants to verify the network. Today, validators risk their ETH to help secure this agreement. This is different from miners who put electricity and computational power to work. The main difference is the mechanism used to agree on new blocks. Ethereum kept its accounts, applications, and transaction history intact.
