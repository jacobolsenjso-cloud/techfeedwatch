The supply chain for artificial intelligence (AI) chips faces significant vulnerabilities, primarily stemming from a heavy global reliance on manufacturing in China for Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs). These essential components form the physical foundation for virtually all modern electronics, including the most advanced AI processors. This dependency creates national security concerns, economic risks, and potential for malicious component integration, prompting a strategic push to re-establish domestic production capabilities in the United States.

## The Foundation of AI: Understanding Printed Circuit Boards

Printed Circuit Boards are the unsung heroes of the electronics world, serving as the base underneath every single chip in devices ranging from smartphones to fighter jets and the powerful server racks that drive AI. Far from being simple pieces of plastic, PCBs are highly complex, technical infrastructure. They are meticulously designed with etched blueprints where chips and other circuitry are mounted, enabling electrical signals to travel and components to communicate.

Modern PCBs can be incredibly intricate, sometimes comprising up to 140 layers. This multi-layered construction is vital for creating the dense pathways required for multiple chips to interact efficiently within advanced electronics. As the physical limits of increasing chip complexity (often referred to as Moore's Law) are approached, the ability to combine numerous chips on sophisticated PCBs becomes even more critical for advancing AI capabilities. The manufacturing process for a PCB is extensive, involving up to a thousand different steps. It begins with thin layers of copper and resin epoxy, which are then carefully aligned and pressed together under heat and pressure for three to five hours. Designs are created using lithography, and thousands of precise holes, some as small as 0.04mm—thinner than a human hair—are drilled to form interconnect pathways. While a simple board might take five to ten days to produce, a highly complex one can require up to six months. The cost of these boards varies widely, from single digits to $100,000, depending on their complexity and the precious metals, such as gold or palladium, used in their construction.

## A Critical Dependency: China's Dominance in PCB Manufacturing

Historically, PCBs were a less visible part of the chipmaking ecosystem, often viewed as a commoditized product. However, their significance has surged dramatically in recent years, driven largely by the explosive demand for AI chips and the increased needs of defense sectors. Despite this heightened importance, the global manufacturing situation for PCBs is heavily skewed. Today, six out of ten printed circuit boards are made in mainland China, and nearly all advanced AI circuit boards are produced there.

This represents a stark shift from the year 2000, when America produced 30% of the world's supply of printed circuit boards; that figure has now plummeted to 4%. The primary driver behind China's dominance is cost. Manufacturing PCBs is a labor-intensive process, and labor is significantly less expensive in China. Furthermore, Chinese manufacturers benefit from substantial government subsidies, enabling them to invest in equipment and capital in ways that U.S. companies often cannot. The sheer scale of operations in Asia is also unmatched; factories in China can house 100 or even 200 PCB assembly lines, a scale not seen in the United States. For instance, one of TTM's China factories is 46 times the size of its original facility in Santa Ana, California. This cost advantage extends to raw materials, which are often cheaper and more readily available from a broader network of suppliers in Asia.

## National Security and Supply Chain Risks

The overwhelming reliance on foreign manufacturing, particularly in China, for such foundational components introduces substantial national security and supply chain risks for the United States. For critical applications, such as those used by the U.S. Department of Defense, circuit boards must be made domestically to prevent the introduction of malicious components. PCBs offer multiple avenues for attack by a potential adversary. Malicious actors could embed hidden switches within the board's many layers or substrates, potentially routing sensitive data back to a foreign entity or even installing "kill switches" that could disable systems at a time and place of their choosing.

The worst-case scenarios are dire: a PCB installed in a weapons platform, like a missile or drone, could be compromised to disrupt its guidance, causing it to land in the wrong location, hit unintended targets, or even damage the launch platform itself. Experts suggest that the PCB level is one of the easiest points to disrupt an electronics chain due to the intricate interconnects and layered construction. Beyond security, the current supply chain faces significant economic vulnerabilities. The dual demand signals from AI data centers and defense have put immense pressure on global PCB production. Raw material availability is a growing concern; conflicts, such as those in the Middle East, can disrupt the supply of critical materials like resin, while the U.S. may have only a single source for other vital components like copper foil. The industry is also dependent on specialized manufacturing equipment, and a breakdown in any part of this complex machinery could cripple production. These factors have led to soaring prices, with PCB costs reportedly rising by up to 40% from March to April, and some manufacturers raising their prices by 5% to 25%.

## Rebuilding Domestic Capacity: U.S. Initiatives and Challenges

In response to these growing concerns, the U.S. government and domestic manufacturers are working to rebuild and strengthen the national PCB industry. Legislative efforts include the Protecting Circuit Boards and Substrates Act, which proposes a 25% tax credit for companies that choose American-made circuit boards and calls for $3 billion in grants for U.S. manufacturers. While this funding is significantly less than the $52.7 billion allocated by the CHIPS Act for semiconductor fabs, building PCB plants is also considerably less expensive, typically costing up to $400 million compared to over $10 billion for a chip fabrication plant.

The goal is to de-risk and diversify the supply chain by incentivizing domestic production and creating trusted sources. Companies like TTM, one of the largest U.S.-based PCB manufacturers, are expanding their domestic footprint. TTM operates 16 plants in the U.S. and is opening new facilities, including a 200,000 square foot factory in Syracuse, New York, specifically for defense products, and a 750,000 square foot plant in Wisconsin that could be used for AI boards. To compete with the cost advantages of Asian manufacturing, U.S. companies are investing heavily in automation, utilizing equipment that can drill 100,000 holes per minute and employing automated optical inspection to replace manual visual checks. While major tech companies like Nvidia mitigate risk through physical inspections, X-rays, and AI-enabled image detection of incoming PCBs, the long-term strategy involves establishing a competitive domestic industry that provides viable alternatives to foreign sourcing.

## The Path Forward for a Secure AI Future

Securing the AI chip supply chain requires a concerted effort to foster a resilient and trusted domestic PCB industry. The strategic shift away from a near-total reliance on a single region is seen as essential for national security and economic stability. This involves not only government incentives and corporate investment in new facilities and automation but also embracing innovation. Startups are exploring new approaches, such as fluid circuit boards that can be rapidly rewired or using AI to accelerate the design of increasingly complex boards.

The vision for the coming years is to see a significant increase in PCB production within the U.S. and Europe, including for advanced AI applications. This effort aims to provide American firms with options to purchase domestically from trusted partners, thereby reducing vulnerabilities and ensuring the integrity and availability of the foundational components for future AI development. The challenge lies in making domestic production cost-competitive against heavily subsidized foreign alternatives, but the long-term benefits of a secure and diversified supply chain are considered paramount.
