Activist investing is a strategy where shareholders acquire a meaningful ownership stake in a company with the explicit goal of influencing its management or strategic direction. These investors typically identify companies they believe are undervalued or underperforming due to suboptimal strategies, operational inefficiencies, or poor corporate governance. Their interventions aim to drive specific changes that they believe will enhance shareholder value and improve the company's long-term prospects.

## Identifying Opportunities for Intervention

Activist investors, often referred to as "value investors," actively seek out companies where they believe there is a disconnect between the company's intrinsic worth and its market valuation. This often occurs during periods of market volatility, which can create opportunities to buy positions at opportunistic prices. They look for situations where a company's stock has "dislocated," meaning its price has fallen more than its underlying quality or growth prospects would suggest. For instance, an activist investor might target a company whose stock has dropped significantly, perhaps from 90 to 55 or 60, after an earnings report, believing the market reaction to be an overcorrection.

Targets for activist campaigns frequently include companies with clear operational inefficiencies, such as those struggling with high costs in their core processes. Another common scenario involves businesses with strong fundamentals or a competitive advantage, sometimes described as having a "moat," but which are temporarily "in the penalty box" due to past missteps like over-expansion or supply chain issues. Activists also focus on companies with significant growth potential that is not being fully realized, often due to a reluctance to adopt new strategies or expand into new markets.

## Strategies for Driving Corporate Change

Once an activist investor has identified a target company, they employ various strategies to influence its direction. These can range from direct engagement with management and the board to more public campaigns. A primary approach involves advocating for strategic shifts, such as pushing for new growth models or challenging existing expansion plans. For example, an activist might suggest a company that currently owns all its domestic operations should consider layering on a franchising model to accelerate growth, especially if it already successfully franchises internationally or in non-traditional venues like airports.

Operational improvements are another common focus. This might involve scrutinizing a company's cost structure, identifying areas for greater efficiency in purchasing, manufacturing, or service delivery. Activists may also advocate for changes in corporate governance, which can include appointing new, independent board members or even pushing for the replacement of key executives, including the CEO. In some cases, activist campaigns have led to sweeping changes across an entire board of directors. The goal is always to align the company's leadership and operations more closely with strategies designed to maximize shareholder returns.

## Real-World Examples of Activist Campaigns

Activist firm Starboard Value, with over $9 billion in assets, has engaged in numerous campaigns illustrating these strategies.

One recent example is **Shake Shack**, where Starboard has taken a position worth several hundred million dollars, making it one of the largest active shareholders. Starboard identified Shake Shack as a high-quality business with significant growth potential, currently valued at roughly 10 times its EBITDA, which they believe should be twice that given its mid-teens growth rate. While acknowledging the company is well-run operationally, Starboard's primary push is to accelerate growth by introducing U.S. franchising. Shake Shack currently owns its approximately 400 U.S. restaurants but franchises internationally and in non-traditional locations. Starboard believes that by layering on U.S. franchising, Shake Shack could reach its target of 1500 stores in seven years instead of 12 years, significantly boosting its growth rate and valuation. The company's historical stance was to own all U.S. restaurants to control quality, but Starboard argues that Shake Shack has already proven independent operators can maintain its high standards in other contexts.

Another campaign involved **CarMax**, where Starboard acquired shares when the company was trading at book value, which they considered a cheap multiple of earnings and cash flow. Starboard identified that CarMax was struggling to regain market share because its prices to consumers were too high. This was attributed to inefficiencies in buying and refurbishing cars, where the company might have added around $2000 on top of the cost, making the final price uncompetitive. Starboard advocated for greater efficiency in these processes and successfully pushed for a new CEO and new board members, some of whom they recommended. The company has since begun to implement these changes, showing promising signs of improved performance and momentum.

**Lamb Weston**, a premier French fry company, also saw Starboard's intervention. The company had faced challenges, including supply issues, after expanding too quickly, placing it "in the penalty box" with investors. Starboard's involvement led to the elevation of the CEO and the appointment of a new executive chair. Since then, Lamb Weston's stock has performed better, volumes have recovered, and earnings have stabilized. Discussions continue regarding how to further improve the earnings growth trajectory.

Starboard's past successes also include well-known campaigns at **Papa John's**, where the firm was involved in operational changes and hired a new CEO, and **Darden Restaurants**, a campaign that famously involved detailed critiques of operational aspects and resulted in significant board changes.

## Measuring the Impact of Activist Engagement

The impact of activist investing can be substantial, often leading to tangible improvements in a company's financial performance and market standing. Successful campaigns can result in significant increases in stock value, as seen with Lamb Weston and CarMax after Starboard's involvement. By pushing for accelerated growth strategies, such as the proposed franchising model for Shake Shack, activists aim to increase a company's growth rate, which can lead to higher valuation multiples.

Beyond stock performance, activist interventions frequently drive greater operational efficiency, leading to improved profitability. The focus on cost reduction and process optimization, as seen in the CarMax example, directly contributes to a stronger bottom line. Changes in corporate governance, including new leadership and board members, can bring fresh perspectives and expertise, guiding the company toward more effective long-term strategies. Ultimately, the goal is to create a higher return for shareholders by addressing underperformance and realizing a company's full potential.

## Potential Challenges and Criticisms

While activist investing can be a powerful force for corporate improvement, it is not without its challenges and potential criticisms. Management teams may initially resist proposed changes, especially if they conflict with long-held corporate philosophies or established operational models. For instance, Shake Shack's historical decision to own all its U.S. restaurants to control quality, while a sound strategy initially, presented a point of discussion for activists advocating for franchising.

The process of implementing activist-driven changes can also be complex and time-consuming. It requires extensive engagement, data analysis, and negotiation to convince existing leadership and shareholders of the merits of the proposed strategies. There is also the perception, sometimes accurate, that activist campaigns can be short-term focused, prioritizing immediate financial gains over long-term strategic health. However, many successful campaigns, like those described, demonstrate a focus on fundamental improvements in operations, governance, and growth strategy that aim to create sustainable value. The success of any activist campaign ultimately hinges on the validity of its proposed changes and the ability to effectively implement them within the company.
