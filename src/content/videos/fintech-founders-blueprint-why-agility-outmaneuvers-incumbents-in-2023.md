The fintech sector is undergoing a large transformation, moving beyond its peak investment period to emphasize sustainable business models. While the rapid growth seen in previous years has recalibrated, large opportunities remain for companies that can address real-world financial challenges with innovative, customer-centric solutions. Both new startups and traditional financial institutions are navigating this evolving environment, with success depending on strategic agility and a clear path to profitability.

## The Evolving Fintech Environment

The fintech industry has experienced remarkable growth, reaching an all-time high before investments tapered in 2022. This shift reflects a broader market correction where the previous "growth at all costs" mentality is being replaced by a focus on cost management and profitability. This recalibration is a healthy development for the long-term sustainability and viability of the sector, encouraging more strong and efficient business practices.

## Large Opportunities for New Entrants

Despite the changing investment climate, starting a fintech company remains a valuable endeavor due to persistent gaps in the financial sector. Globally, 1.4 billion adults still lack access to banking services, presenting a major opportunity for financial inclusion. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) face a large funding shortfall, with a global gap of about $5.2 trillion between demand for financing and available funds from traditional sources. In Africa, this SME funding gap is estimated to be a large amount.

Beyond these quantitative needs, there are also qualitative gaps. Many traditional financial institutions offer a customer experience that falls short of modern expectations. There is a clear demand for more innovative, user-friendly, and intuitive solutions that prioritize convenience and ease of use, especially among younger generations. New fintech companies can thrive by creating unique value propositions that solve these specific problems, rather than simply replicating existing services. Defining the core problem, identifying the target market, and establishing a clear monetization strategy are essential first steps. Market research is then vital to validate these assumptions and ensure there is a sufficient "market in the gap" to support a viable business.

## Building a Fintech Startup: Key Steps

Launching a successful fintech startup requires careful planning and execution. After defining a unique idea and business model, founders must conduct thorough market research. This research helps validate the concept, analyze competitors, and identify unmet needs that are large enough to sustain a business.

Understanding the regulatory environment is equally important. The financial services sector is heavily regulated, and laws vary much by country. Founders must be aware of data protection, Know Your Customer (KYC), anti-money laundering (AML), and consumer protection policies. Some territories may require specific operating licenses, minimum capital, or a proof of concept. Regulatory sandboxes offer a way to test new products within a controlled environment. Ignoring regulations can lead to severe consequences, as seen when authorities have banned certain crypto operations.

Developing a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) is the next practical step. An MVP includes the essential features needed to solve the identified problem. This approach allows for cost-effective testing, gathers early customer feedback, and serves as a tangible demonstration for potential investors or team members. With an MVP in place, building the right team becomes critical. This includes hiring skilled developers, designers, and marketing professionals. Careful selection of initial team members is important, and founders should ensure personal relationships do not hinder business objectives.

Finally, a well-defined marketing strategy is necessary to reach the target audience, using tactics like content marketing, social media, and influencer outreach. Securing funding, whether from angel investors, venture capitalists, crowdfunding, or bank loans, often follows. A solid business plan and financial projections that demonstrate growth potential are key to attracting investment. The process does not end with launch; continuous iteration and improvement based on customer feedback are essential for long-term success.

## Incumbents' Approach: Fintech Subsidiaries

Traditional banks are increasingly venturing into the fintech space by establishing their own subsidiaries. This strategy is driven by several factors. Banks face competitive pressure from agile fintech startups that threaten to disrupt traditional services, particularly in payments and lending. Creating subsidiaries can also offer a way to innovate and compete more effectively, sometimes by operating with greater freedom from the strict regulatory restrictions that apply to the parent bank. And, there is growing customer demand for digital-first and mobile-first banking services that offer convenience, security, and ease of use.

Several banks have found success with this model. Examples include Fincer by Equity Bank in Kenya, Alat by Wema Bank in Nigeria, Marcus by Goldman Sachs, Finn by JPMorgan Chase, and Mox by Standard Chartered Hong Kong. These subsidiaries aim to combine the stability and resources of a large bank with the agility and innovation of a startup.

## Why Bank-Led Fintechs Can Struggle

Despite the potential, many bank-led fintech subsidiaries struggle to achieve their goals. A common pitfall is the failure to establish a distinct culture separate from the parent bank. Traditional banks often have bureaucratic processes and a different mindset, which can stifle the speed and innovation needed for a fintech startup. For instance, many fintechs take 5 or more years to reach profitability; some successful ones like Alipay and PayPal took 3 years, Revolut took 4 years, Stripe took 6 years, and Coinbase took 9 years. If a bank imposes short-term profitability expectations, it can hinder the subsidiary's development.

Another challenge is insufficient autonomy. If the bank focuses too much on minor iterations of its existing services, it limits the subsidiary's ability to explore new areas of growth. Fintechs need the freedom to be creative and operate independently. Attracting the right talent is also difficult. Fintech startups require different skill sets and mindsets than traditional banking roles, and banks may struggle to compete with the flexible employment packages and perks offered by other tech companies.

A lack of flexibility can also impede success. Fintechs often need to pivot quickly in response to market changes or evolving customer needs. If the parent bank does not understand or support this adaptability, the subsidiary becomes less nimble. Finally, misaligned goals can be a large issue. Fintech startups often prioritize customer experience and are willing to take risks to meet those needs. They may use different metrics for success than traditional banks. If the bank does not adjust its expectations and adopt a similar mindset, it can lead to the subsidiary's failure. Recognizing and addressing these challenges is essential for banks to build successful fintech subsidiaries that can truly compete in the fast-paced financial market.
