The foundational mechanics behind Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT are complex, involving massive data ingestion and intricate neural network processes. A closer examination reveals how these systems acquire their remarkable abilities and why they sometimes exhibit unexpected behaviors.

At their core, LLMs are predictive engines. They undergo a "pretraining" phase, consuming petabytes of text and code from the internet, learning to predict the next token in a sequence. This statistical learning builds a vast internal representation of language, including grammatical rules, factual associations, and stylistic patterns. The concept of "tokenization" is central to this, where text breaks down into numerical tokens, forming the input and output for the neural network. This process, while efficient, also underpins certain model struggles, like difficulty with spelling or nuanced reasoning due to abstract token representations rather than character-level understanding.

Following pretraining, models undergo "post-training," a refinement stage that adapts them for specific tasks, often through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). SFT uses labeled conversation data to teach the model how to respond in a dialogue format, while RLHF employs human preferences to guide the model towards more helpful, harmless, and honest outputs. This iterative feedback loop is crucial for mitigating issues like factual inaccuracies, often termed "hallucinations," and aligning the model's behavior with human expectations. While these methods significantly improve usability, the models still operate on statistical likelihoods, leading to moments of "jagged intelligence" where capabilities appear inconsistent.
