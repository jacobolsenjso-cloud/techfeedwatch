Yes, smartphones can explode. However, severe battery failures remain rare during normal daily use.

Explosions happen when an internal short circuit triggers thermal runaway. Thermal runaway is a self-heating chain reaction that produces intense heat and fire. Modern mobile devices pack high energy densities (large amounts of stored energy) into tight frames.

These frames are made of aluminum and glass. When factory errors, physical damage, or high heat break internal separators, that energy escapes rapidly as fire.

## Why do smartphone batteries catch fire and explode?

Smartphone batteries explode when internal short circuits spark thermal runaway. Under normal conditions, a porous plastic separator keeps internal components apart.

It separates the positive and negative electrodes (parts where electricity enters and leaves). If that barrier tears, energy surges through the electrolyte fluid (the liquid conducting electricity) all at once.

According to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), thermal runaway in lithium-ion batteries can produce extreme flame temperatures. These flames can reach up to 1,100 degrees Celsius (2,012 degrees Fahrenheit), according to NIST.

The reaction feeds itself by breaking down internal chemical bonds and generating oxygen. That process produces rapid pressure spikes that rupture metal casings.

Factory assembly flaws represent one primary cause of battery failure. According to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), Samsung recalled approximately 1.9 million Galaxy Note 7 smartphones in October 2016.

That action followed 96 reports of overheating batteries in the United States, according to the CPSC. These incidents included 13 burn injuries and 47 reports of property damage, according to the CPSC.

According to engineering consulting firm Exponent and device maker [Samsung Electronics](https://www.samsung.com/us/), technicians traced the fires to two distinct manufacturing defects. These flaws occurred across different suppliers.

Those flaws included negative electrode deflection (bending) in cell corners, according to Exponent and Samsung Electronics. Sharp internal welding burrs (rough metal edges) also punctured the thin separator layer, according to Exponent and Samsung Electronics.

Despite headline incidents, catastrophic failures do not occur often. According to [CNET](https://www.cnet.com/), baseline manufacturing quality control limits typical lithium-ion cell failure rates to roughly one in 10 million. Everyday wear or factory shortcuts create the primary risks.

- Severe physical impacts that compress the battery pouch
- Microscopic manufacturing burrs piercing internal layers
- Extreme environmental heat that degrades safety membranes
- Faulty third-party charging cables delivering unregulated voltages

Combustion (burning) creates severe air quality hazards alongside heat. Lithium-ion batteries emit over 100 toxic gases during combustion. These battery emissions contain high concentrations of carbon monoxide and hydrogen fluoride.

The Fire Department of the City of New York (FDNY) and the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) also documented these hazards.

## Swollen cases and hissing sounds warn of battery failure

A failing smartphone battery gives clear physical warnings. These include chassis (outer frame) expansion, intense heat during idle states, and distinct hissing sounds.

Users should never treat battery gas generation as a minor cosmetic issue. Trapped gases push outward on display assemblies and back panels.

According to PCMag, key physical warning signs of an impending battery explosion include chassis bulging or swelling. Other warning signs include a hot-to-the-touch casing while idle, according to PCMag.

PCMag also notes that plastic-burning or chemical odors and hissing or popping noises warn of danger. These symptoms show that internal layers have started degrading.

According to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), thermal runaway is often preceded by an audible "click-hiss" sound. This occurs when internal pressure ruptures the cell's safety valve, according to NIST.

That distinct sound indicates that pressurized gases have broken the seal. Immediate isolation becomes necessary at that point.

- Disconnect charging cords from wall outlets immediately
- Place the hot phone onto a non-flammable stone or concrete surface
- Keep away from the device to avoid inhaling vented fumes
- Contact qualified local recycling centers for safe disposal

A swollen battery presents real fire risks in homes and workplaces. Knowing [why your iPhone gets hot](/video/why-is-my-iphone-hot-and-how-ios-regulates-heat) helps separate normal processor warmth from dangerous hardware breakdown. Acting quickly prevents property destruction and serious skin burns.

My background as a fire and evacuation instructor taught me that keeping a calm overview during sudden hazards makes all the difference. When hardware starts hissing or bulging, rushing around in a panic only increases the risk of trouble. Giving the device space and moving it onto a safe surface right away is simply sensible practice.

## How can you prevent your phone from overheating?

Avoid extreme temperatures, use certified charging gear, and replace damaged or bent batteries immediately. Lithium-ion packs perform best in moderate ambient (surrounding) conditions. Leaving a handset on a vehicle dashboard bakes delicate battery components.

Demanding apps also generate warmth that taxes internal circuits. Playing intensive games while connected to fast chargers concentrates heat in tiny spaces. Giving hardware space to cool down prevents thermal stress on battery separators.

Hard drops against asphalt can crease internal battery layers without shattering exterior glass. If your device dents near the power pack, watch its temperature closely. Hardware deformation (loss of original shape) remains a common cause of delayed short circuits.

According to UL Standards & Engagement, thermal runaway incidents in commercial aviation rose 15 percent in 2025. These events reached an average of 2.36 incidents per week, according to UL Standards & Engagement. UL Standards & Engagement documented that 12 percent of those events forced flight diversions or emergency landings.
